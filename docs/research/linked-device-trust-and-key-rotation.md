# Linked-device trust and key rotation for Pomo

## Question

What should Pomo borrow or avoid from proven linked-device and group-key protocols when a single member has one to three equal, intermittently connected Android and Chrome replicas?

The comparison is intentionally narrower than a general cryptography survey. It covers stable member identity versus device keys, admission, attribution, concurrent offline trust changes, revocation, historical-key sharing, forward content-key rotation, recovery rotation, stale recovery, and the availability assumptions each design makes.

## Recommendation

Pomo should keep its proposed **stable Member ID + independent Device Identities + signed authorization journal + versioned Content epochs**. No surveyed protocol is a drop-in authorization layer for an offline peer system.

Borrow these mechanisms:

1. **Separate device signing and agreement keys.** MLS requires distinct signature and encryption keys in group leaves; Keybase independently provisions device signing and encryption keys. This gives Pomo attributable Operations and a clean target for wrapping Content-epoch secrets.
2. **Mutually authenticated, short-lived device admission.** Keybase's KEX has the new device prove its new key, the existing device counter-sign a constrained admission statement, and the endpoints authenticate an encrypted channel with a QR/manual secret. Matrix QR verification similarly fixes the exact master/device public keys at the start of the ceremony. Signal uses a QR-carried provisioning address and public key, but relies on its primary device and service.
3. **Rotate a member content key on revocation and wrap it separately to every remaining device.** Keybase's Per-User Key (PUK) generations are the closest match to Pomo's proposed Content epochs.
4. **Make retained-history access explicit.** Keybase encrypts the previous PUK seed under the next generation, while Matrix stores historical Megolm session keys in an encrypted server-side backup. Both demonstrate that forward revocation and historical recovery are separate policies.
5. **Keep recovery authority distinct from routine device signing.** Matrix separates a cross-signing identity from device keys and can store its private material in Secret Storage. Pomo should go further: a stable genesis-derived Member ID must survive Recovery-authority rotation.

Do not inherit these constraints:

- **MLS's single linear epoch sequence.** Concurrent Commits for one epoch conflict and require application-level canonicalization, commonly an ordering service. That is incompatible with making two offline Pomo trust changes independently valid and later causally reconcilable.
- **Matrix's homeserver account as the ultimate admission/recovery authority.** Cross-signing proves device trust, but device creation/deletion and current account data remain server operations; resetting cross-signing changes the cryptographic identity and requires warnings and re-verification.
- **Signal's permanent primary device and service-owned device registry.** Current server code allows only the primary to create a link token, and current Desktop provisioning imports account identity private keys. This is smooth for a centralized messenger but defeats equal replicas and per-device cryptographic attribution.
- **Keybase's globally ordered server-hosted sigchain.** Its device and PUK design is useful, but concurrent offline trust changes cannot both append at the same `seqno`/`prev`; a server transaction and Merkle-published current chain resolve order.

## Primary-source findings

### MLS: borrow the key schedule, not the membership log

MLS deliberately separates an Authentication Service from a Delivery Service and treats each client as a group member ([RFC 9420, protocol overview](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-3)). Additions use a `KeyPackage`, Commit, and encrypted Welcome; a new member receives the new group state but cannot access messages from before it joined ([RFC 9420 section 3.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-3.2)). Removal advances the epoch with fresh entropy unavailable to the removed member ([RFC 9420 section 3.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-3.2)). Its leaf encryption and signature keys must be distinct ([RFC 9420 section 16.7](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-16.7)). These are good precedents for Pomo's separate Device keys and forward Content-epoch rotation.

The decisive mismatch is sequencing. A Commit is premised on exactly one starting epoch. If two members concurrently produce Commits from that epoch, the application must prevent the conflict or choose a canonical winner ([RFC 9420 section 14](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-14)). The RFC even notes that a sender may advance early only if it knows its Commit will be next, for example by an orchestration-server promise ([RFC 9420 section 3.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-3.2)). The MLS architecture's eventual-consistency guidance still resolves concurrent Commits by choosing a winner and abandoning/rolling back the other state; it does not merge both transitions ([RFC 9750 section 5.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9750#section-5.2)). MLS therefore does not solve Pomo's causal union/quarantine rules for two independently offline device-trust changes.

MLS normally needs no member quorum, but availability still depends on canonical ordering and delivery. The RFC describes optional acknowledgements from members covering the UpdatePath to reduce malformed-Commit lockout; that mitigation is explicitly an added availability tradeoff ([RFC 9420 section 16.12](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9420#section-16.12)). Pomo's one-to-three trusted devices should not require that quorum.

### Matrix: borrow layered trust and recoverable ciphertext

Matrix cross-signing places a master signing key above replaceable self-signing and user-signing keys; the self-signing key signs the user's device keys ([Matrix Client-Server specification, Cross-signing](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#cross-signing)). Its QR verification modes bind the transaction, the exact master/device public keys, and a random shared secret ([Matrix Client-Server specification, QR codes](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#qr-codes)). Clients are expected to warn on master-key changes, and non-cross-signed devices should not receive encrypted secrets by default. This usefully separates “the server says this device belongs to the account” from “the user's cryptographic trust says this device is authorized.”

Secure Secret Storage can hold cross-signing private keys and the room-key-backup decryption key under a default recovery key; an existing verified device can alternatively share secrets over an encrypted to-device channel ([Matrix Client-Server specification, Secure Secret Storage and Sharing](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#secure-secret-storage-and-sharing)). Server-side key backup stores encrypted historical session keys and does not require another device to be online, while retaining `forwarded_count` and `is_verified` metadata ([Matrix Client-Server specification, Server-side key backups](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#server-side-key-backups)). The current backup is versioned, rejects writes to the wrong version, and exposes an `etag` that lets a client notice another writer ([Matrix room-key backup API](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#get_matrixclientv3room_keysversion)). Pomo should similarly offer both verified-device transfer and Recovery-backed asynchronous retrieval, and bind key-envelope/checkpoint manifests to an explicit version and causal frontier.

Matrix is not an offline peer trust ledger. Devices and access tokens are homeserver resources; deleting a device invalidates its access token and should require extra server authentication ([Matrix Client-Server specification, device management](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/client-server-api/#device-management)). If Secret Storage/cross-signing material is lost, clients may reset Secret Storage, cross-signing, and backup, after which other devices/users must react to the changed master key. Pomo needs a stable genesis Member ID and explicit Recovery-authority generation instead of equating a replaceable cross-signing root with permanent identity.

### Signal: borrow pairing ergonomics, not primary authority

Sesame is a strong design for asynchronous per-device message sessions under loss, duplication, reordering, state deletion, and rollback. It explicitly assumes a server that stores the current user/device record and temporary per-device mailboxes, and it permits either per-user or per-device identity keys ([Signal Sesame specification](https://signal.org/docs/specifications/sesame/)). That is message-session repair, not reconciliation of concurrent offline authorization histories.

Signal's current product uses a registered primary device: official guidance says linking/relinking happens from the primary and that the primary must periodically come online ([Signal linked-device documentation](https://support.signal.org/hc/en-us/articles/360007320551-Linked-Devices)). The current server implementation only lets the primary mint the linking token, allows the primary to remove linked devices, and records the new device in the service account ([Signal Server `DeviceController`](https://github.com/signalapp/Signal-Server/blob/7e863e9dbaca532e45b42ba53433defcf3245224/service/src/main/java/org/whispersystems/textsecuregcm/controllers/DeviceController.java#L149-L305)). The server relays an encrypted provisioning message from the existing account device to a QR-named temporary address ([Signal Server `ProvisioningController`](https://github.com/signalapp/Signal-Server/blob/7e863e9dbaca532e45b42ba53433defcf3245224/service/src/main/java/org/whispersystems/textsecuregcm/controllers/ProvisioningController.java#L38-L103)).

Representative Desktop source shows that provisioning currently imports private ACI/PNI identity material, plus account/profile/backup secrets, into the linked installation ([Signal Desktop `ProvisioningCipher.node.ts`](https://github.com/signalapp/Signal-Desktop/blob/de8fe1e7084fbab9c4e9c667c2d0ec0f208d1adc/ts/textsecure/ProvisioningCipher.node.ts#L31-L139)). Pomo should copy the encrypted QR ceremony's usability, but the new device must generate its own signing/agreement keys and receive only wrapped Content-epoch material.

### Keybase: the closest key hierarchy, with the wrong ordering dependency

Keybase devices keep local device keys advertised in a user's signature chain, while paper keys act as recovery devices. Its PUK seed is boxed to every active device. On revocation, an active device creates a fresh generation and boxes it only to remaining devices ([Keybase Per-User Keys](https://book.keybase.io/docs/teams/puk)). The new generation also encrypts the prior seed, supporting retained-history traversal from the newest authorized generation; the upstream implementation derives a dedicated previous-generation key and seals/opens the old seed ([Keybase `per_user_key.go`](https://github.com/keybase/client/blob/527fae6d5389e2899c41c5bdcb5b7097e4e5eb50/go/libkb/per_user_key.go#L44-L150)). This is the closest precedent for Pomo Content epochs.

Keybase KEX is also a useful admission model. The new device contributes and proves its new signing/agreement keys; the existing device verifies a constrained statement, counter-signs it, and boxes the current PUK to the new device over an authenticated channel bootstrapped by a QR/manual secret ([Keybase KEX protocol](https://book.keybase.io/docs/crypto/key-exchange)). Pomo can replace Keybase's passphrase/session transfer and server RPC with a short-lived pairing channel plus one durable `AuthorizeDevice` Operation.

The incompatibility is the account sigchain. Every link has one sequence number and previous-link hash, and clients replay that ordered chain to determine valid sibling keys ([Keybase server and sigchain design](https://book.keybase.io/docs/server)). KEX ends by posting related signatures/key boxes for atomic server acceptance. The public Merkle tree makes server rollback detectable, but it also identifies the current accepted chain. Pomo has no central append authority, so it must retain its causal authorization journal and deterministic conflict/quarantine semantics rather than adopting Keybase's sigchain verbatim.

## Protocol boundary for Pomo

The research supports the following boundary for the implementation specification:

- **Identity plane:** immutable genesis record → stable Member ID; independently rotatable Recovery authority; independent Device signing/agreement certificates.
- **Authorization plane:** signed causal `AuthorizeDevice`, `RevokeDevice`, `RotateRecovery`, and `RecoveryReset` Operations. Authorization decisions are materialized from causal context, not mailbox arrival time or wall clock.
- **Content plane:** fresh random Content-epoch secrets, named by the authorization transition/frontier they follow and wrapped to current Device agreement keys plus current Recovery authority. More than one epoch may temporarily exist while offline branches reconcile; the materializer must not pretend there was a single MLS-style next Commit.
- **History plane:** retained historical epochs are shared explicitly with a newly admitted Full device. Revocation rotates forward and never promises to erase previously obtained plaintext. Recovery files identify their Recovery-authority generation and known authorization frontier; stale generations fail closed.
- **Availability:** no routine quorum and no permanent primary. One accepted Full device can admit or revoke; current Recovery authority can perform a reset. An offline Operation can be locally durable, but its effect on other replicas and any concurrent trust conflict cannot be declared globally settled until anti-entropy runs.

## Open specification risks

This research does not choose cryptographic algorithms or resolve the later domain rules. The implementation specification still needs to define:

1. How overlapping Content epochs are named, distributed, and superseded after concurrent offline revocations/admissions.
2. Whether a device may encrypt ordinary additive Operations under an older but causally valid epoch after another device has concurrently rotated forward.
3. Exact limits on retained historical epoch keys and how checkpoints remain decryptable after compaction.
4. Canonical encoding, signature context strings, KDF labels, key-wrapping algorithms, passphrase KDF parameters, and platform keystore behavior. These require a focused cryptographic design review rather than copying any protocol's primitives piecemeal.
